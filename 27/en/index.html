Introduction

With Our Backs to the Future

Anyone who has ever rented a rowboat knows that the rower sits with his back to the direction in which he is moving. He cannot see the place he is rowing toward, yet he pulls on the oars with all his strength. His eyes are fixed on the wake he leaves behind him, and to keep a straight course he picks a point on the shore, a tree or a bridge, and makes sure the stern of the boat stays pointed at it. This is how we move through time. The future we are sailing toward lies behind our backs, and everything we understand about reality rests on what we have already passed through. Our gaze wanders among moments of the past, asking how each of them shifted our course. Where were you when Rabin was murdered? What did you do in the days of the Gaza disengagement? Did you go out to Rothschild Boulevard in the summer of the cost-of-living protest? Did you catch COVID? Where were you on Gallant Night? Where did October 7 find you? Each of these singular points, and the way we responded to it, has shaped our journey and will continue to shape it.

Since January 2023, thousands of speeches have been delivered from protest stages: at Habima Square and on Kaplan Street, at junctions and on bridges, from Eilat to Kiryat Shmona. Along the way we collected some of them in twenty-six booklets, “Speak Out”, “Back to the Streets” and “Staying in the Streets”, and illustrated them with photographs by the women and men of the Photographers’ Patrol and the Drone Patrol. On the eve of the elections, instead of another booklet of speeches, we went back to all the booklets and sampled short, powerful pieces from them – a sentence, a paragraph or a photograph – that touch the heart of the matter and our own hearts, and we set them on a timeline beside the events in whose shadow they were spoken. This is the shore we look at from the boat.

Read in sequence, the pieces and the photographs come together into a single story. They tell us that there are innocent people and there are criminals in every place and in every society, and that a better future depends not on another dunam of land but on education, on integrity rather than corruption, on law rather than chaos. A bereaved mother who spoke in Beer Sheva, a cardiologist from Haifa who gathered his department’s staff, Jews and Arabs, on the morning after the massacre, a teacher from a demolished village in the Negev, and a mathematician facing a class of three hundred students – all of them speak from the same faith in humanity, and none of them gives up on hope. In May 2024, Einav Zangauker lit a torch of hope for her son Matan, who was then on his 220th day in captivity. In October 2025, Matan came home. This is the story of an entire camp, one that believes in the human being as a human being, and in the hope that the human spirit will prevail.

Those who stood at the protests did not know that in Gaza, in captivity, they were being seen. Hostages who came back told us that the protests reached them and gave them strength. “I saw you,” one of them said, “that’s what kept me going.” This is hope: to row without seeing the destination, and to believe that every pull on the oars brings us closer to it. This coming Tuesday we will stand in the voting booth with our backs to the future, and the pieces in this booklet will be the point we steer by. October 7 met us without our choosing it. On October 27, the choice is ours. And we will choose hope.

Afterword

Rowing On

We did not take to the streets to write history. We went out because we felt that something fundamental in our country was in danger. In January 2023 we spoke about the separation of powers, about human rights and about democracy. Then came October 7, and the familiar words gave way to the names and faces of people who were murdered, who were kidnapped, who did not come home. The protest changed, and we changed along with it.

In these pages there are thousands of faces and hundreds of moments, but one story keeps returning: the story of people who chose not to stand aside. Some gave speeches, some took photographs, some held a sign in the rain. None of them knew whether anyone was listening, whether there was any point, or whether they would manage to change anything. Sometimes we despaired; sometimes we were angry, even at one another. And still we came back. And on the morning the last living hostages came home, many of us stood in the same square where we had held signs for two years, and this time we wept for joy.

The rower does not see the destination. He picks a point on the shore behind him and keeps to it so as not to drift off course. After almost four years, this is what looking back gives us. It does not show us where we will arrive, but it reminds us what was important enough to leave home and stand together for, and it keeps us on a straight course.

On October 27 we will choose the path along which we continue. The elections will not end this story; they are one more pull on the oars. But some pulls set the direction of everything that comes after them, and this is one of them.

Hope is not a promise that we will reach a safe shore. It is the decision to keep rowing toward it.

On October 27, the choice is ours.
